# Nofit LRT Extension demand study: questions and answers

Plain-language answers to the questions a client is likely to ask. All figures are the
central case of the study (step 45, 5 October 2026): the through line (main route +
extension) with priority at junctions, morning peak 06:00–09:00, unless stated otherwise.
The technical record behind each answer is `METHODOLOGY.md`; the section is given in brackets.

---

## A. The headline

**1. How many people will ride the line?**

About 7,400 trips in the three morning peak hours in 2050 under business-as-usual growth,
of which about 6,000 use the extension (Hamifrats – Haifa – Tirat Carmel). With today's
demand the figure would be about 5,200.

| Year and growth scenario | LRT trips, 06:00–09:00 | Of which on the extension |
|---|---|---|
| 2022 demand | 5,193 | 4,004 |
| 2040, business-as-usual | 6,309 | 4,950 |
| **2050, business-as-usual (reference)** | **7,433** | **6,003** |
| 2040, high growth | 7,476 | 5,651 |
| 2050, high growth | 9,225 | 7,010 |

**2. How sure are you? What is the range?**

The published planning range for 2050 business-as-usual is 5,500 to 10,500 trips. The
central figure is our best single estimate, not a guarantee. The range reflects one main
unknown: how strongly travellers react to a saving in travel time and effort. We could
measure that reaction only roughly from the existing travel survey.

**3. Why are the numbers for the morning peak only? What about the whole day?**

The data and the model are strongest for the morning peak. The evening peak (16:00–19:00)
was also modelled and carries about 80 % of the morning figure (6,135 in 2050
business-as-usual). A whole-day figure, which is what revenue needs, has not been produced
yet.

**4. How busy is the busiest part of the line?**

In the busiest hour of 2050 business-as-usual the whole line carries roughly 4,400 trips,
and the busiest segment between two stations carries about 1,000–1,400 passengers in one
direction across the forecast years. These are upper readings, because the survey shows a
sharper peak than road counts and smart-card data do.

## B. Who rides and why

**5. Where do the riders come from?**

Mostly from today's buses and Metronit. They move to a faster and more reliable vehicle.
Only 6–14 % of the LRT trips come out of cars (818 of 7,433 in 2050 business-as-usual).

**6. Why so few car drivers? Isn't the point to take cars off the road?**

Three reasons:

- The line mainly replaces journeys that public transport already serves, so its largest
  gain is for existing passengers.
- People who own a car and have it available rarely switch in the survey we have. Among
  them only about 2 % use public transport today.
- The model is deliberately cautious here. It does not assume that roads become more
  congested in the future (see question 12).

The car figure is the least certain number in the study. It could plausibly be less than
half or about double. It should not be used for claims on congestion relief or emissions.

**7. Which journeys does the line serve most?**

In 2050 business-as-usual: Tirat Carmel ↔ Haifa 29 %, inside Haifa 22 %, the Krayot
reaching the line by a feeder 22 %, the main route (towards Nazareth) ↔ Haifa 12 %. About
38 % of the trips cross Hamifrats.

**8. Does through-running to Nazareth matter?**

It adds riders to the line as a whole but few to the extension itself. Its main benefit
for extension riders is sparing them a transfer at Hamifrats. The extension operated alone
would carry 5,939 trips in 2050 business-as-usual.

**9. How much does priority at junctions matter?**

A lot. Without priority the line is slower and carries 20–25 % fewer trips (about 5,700
instead of 7,433 in 2050 business-as-usual).

**10. What happens to the Metronit?**

In the central case the Metronit keeps running as today and the two compete on part of
the corridor. We also tested the Metronit serving the Krayot only, up to Merkazit
Hamifrats, with the LRT as the mass-transit mode inside Haifa. The LRT then carries 24–30 %
more (9,570 in 2050 business-as-usual) and its busiest segment 40–50 % more. Public
transport as a whole loses a few hundred trips to the car, because some journeys inside
Haifa lose a Metronit that the LRT does not fully replace.

## C. How the estimate was made

**11. In simple terms, how does the model work?**

1. We built a picture of who travels where today, by car and by public transport, from the
   national travel survey (2017/18), corrected with smart-card ticketing data.
2. We grew that picture to 2040 and 2050 with the official population and employment
   forecasts.
3. For every pair of zones we compared the door-to-door journey by today's bus or Metronit
   with the journey by LRT: walking, waiting, riding and transfers.
4. Where the LRT is better, a share of the travellers moves to it. The bigger the saving,
   the bigger the share.

With no LRT the model returns today's observed travel exactly. This is its main strength:
it starts from what people really do and only estimates the change.

**12. Does the model account for worse road congestion in the future?**

No. Car travel times are held at today's level in every year. If the roads become more
congested by 2050, more people would turn to public transport, and the LRT would carry
more than we show. A first test suggests the effect on the LRT's direct draw from cars is
modest (roughly +10–15 % of the car figure for 10 extra minutes by car), but the overall
public transport market would grow by more. This is one reason the forecast leans to the
cautious side.

**13. Does the model include fares?**

No. It assumes the LRT costs the passenger the same as the bus, which matches the current
integrated fare policy. Parking charges and fuel costs are not modelled either.

**14. Does it include new travel that the line itself creates, or new development around stations?**

Only partly. Population and jobs follow the official forecasts. The model does not add trips that people would make only because the line
exists, and it does not move homes or jobs towards the stations.

**15. Why are there two growth scenarios?**

They are the two official demographic forecasts. Business-as-usual continues current
trends. The high-growth scenario places more of the growth in other parts of the corridor,
for example Matam and the Krayot. The difference between them, about 1,800 trips in 2050,
shows how much the result depends on land-use policy rather than on the line.

## D. Limits and trust

**16. What are the weakest points of the study?**

- **Travellers' sensitivity to time savings** is partly assumed (question 2).
- **The preference for rail over bus** (people usually value a rail ride more than the same
  ride by bus) is an assumption, because no LRT runs in Haifa to measure it.
- **The car shift** (question 6).
- **The travel survey is from 2017/18.** It was updated with ticketing data from 2022 and
  checked against 2025 data, but it predates changes in travel habits since then.
- **No capacity limit.** The model does not check whether the trains can physically carry
  the peak load.

**17. Was the model checked against real data?**

Yes. The base-year picture was tested against the Ministry of Transport's validation
checklist for metropolitan models, against road counts and against smart-card boardings.
The car side matches the counts in total but not road by road, so the study should not be
used to forecast traffic on individual roads.

**18. Can these numbers be used for the business case and statutory approval?**

Not on their own. This is a screening study: it is suited to comparing alternatives,
sizing the market and deciding whether and how to proceed. Formal ridership, revenue and
cost-benefit figures need the full metropolitan transport model, which represents road
congestion, fares, all travel through the area and the whole day.

**19. Can they be used to size the trains and the frequency?**

As a first indication only. The peak loads are upper readings (question 4) and there is no
capacity check. Fleet and frequency decisions need the detailed model.

**20. What would improve the forecast most?**

1. A stated-preference survey: asking travellers in the corridor how they would choose
   between car, bus and LRT. This settles the two main assumptions.
2. Running the project in the metropolitan model, with future road congestion.
3. A whole-day expansion, for revenue.

**21. Can someone else reproduce or change the numbers?**

Yes. Every number is produced by a documented chain that reruns from the raw inputs. A
scenario workbook in Excel (`exports/Nofit_LRT_Scenario_Model.xlsx`) lets a user change
the main assumptions and the Metronit network and see the results follow.

## Terms used

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Extension | The new section Hamifrats – Haifa – Tirat Carmel, 18.7 km, 24 stations |
| Main route | Hamifrats – Nazareth, 40.7 km, under construction |
| Through line | Main route and extension operated as one line |
| Morning peak | 06:00–09:00 on an average weekday |
| Business-as-usual / high growth | The two official population and employment forecasts |
| Priority | The LRT gets a green light at junctions and is not delayed by traffic |
| Screening study | A first estimate for comparing options, before the full model |
