## 2. Synthesis

### 2.1 Recommended slope-aware walk-speed function
Use the r5/OTP convention: a level walking speed chosen for the population, multiplied by Tobler's shape normalised to 1.0 on the flat. This keeps the empirically grounded level speed (1.2–1.4 m/s; S5-09 to S5-13) and borrows only the slope *shape* from Tobler (S5-01; formula verified in r5 and OTP source).

- v(S) = v_flat × exp(−3.5·|S + 0.05|) / exp(−0.175), with S = rise/run (signed, + uphill).
- v_flat: 1.3 m/s base (midpoint of Montufar younger 1.31–1.40 and MUTCD/TCQSM 1.2); sensitivity 1.2 m/s (TCQSM design value) and 1.0 m/s for areas with >20 % elderly (MUTCD/TCQSM, S5-12/13). Haifa's Carmel neighbourhoods have an older age profile, so the 1.0–1.2 m/s case matters there (age profile is my assumption, not from a search).
- Derived time multipliers from the formula (my computation, not a search number): +5 % → 1.19; +10 % → 1.42; +15 % → 1.69; +20 % → 2.01; −5 % → 0.84; −10 % → 1.00; −15 % → 1.19; −20 % → 1.42. This is consistent with the TCQSM statement that grades ≤5 % have little effect and the Human Transit paraphrase that 10 % cuts speed ≈25 % (S5-07/08; unverified).
- Recommended modification for urban use: cap the downhill bonus at 1.0 (no speed-up below flat time). Sun et al. (S5-05) found step length shortens on descent and Kawamura (S5-06) found speed drops at 12° in both directions; Tobler's 6 km/h downhill peak comes from hikers. This cap is my judgement, flagged as such.
- Cross-check: Naismith's rule gives 12 min/km + 10 min per 100 m climb, i.e. a derived uphill multiplier of 1.42 at 5 % and 1.83 at 10 % (steeper than Tobler's 1.19/1.42). Treat Naismith as an upper bound for sensitivity testing.
- Stairs (OSM `highway=steps`): either (a) perceived-time multiplier 1.86 × level walking time (S5-32), or (b) physical speed ≈0.5–0.6 m/s ascending, 0.7–0.9 m/s descending along the stair slope (Fruin, S5-33), which is roughly 2× level time ascending. Use (b) for physical time and (a) as the behavioural weight; do not stack both. Haifa's step-street network (S5-38) makes this a first-order item, not a refinement.
- Alternatives if calibration data appear: Irmischer & Clarke (S5-03, close to a 5th-percentile/slow walker) and Campbell's Lorentz function (S5-04) are both implementable in the same slot; r5 also offers `MINETTI` (energy-cost based) which penalises steep descents, closer to the "cap downhill" intuition.

### 2.2 Crossing delay allowance
- Signalised: HCM d = (C − g)²/(2C), g = WALK + 4 s (S5-15). With Israeli urban cycles (which I could not source; need local signal plans) the formula gives e.g. 27 s at C=90/g=20 and 38 s at C=120/g=25 (derived). HCM LOS bands put typical urban signalised crossings in the 20–40 s (LOS C–D) range (S5-16). Recommendation: 25–30 s per signalised crossing as a central allowance, 15–40 s as the range; a two-stage (median-refuge) crossing should be counted as two.
- Unsignalised/zebra: no reliable seconds figure surfaced; HCM's gap-acceptance method under-predicts observed delay (S5-17). Suggest a nominal 5–10 s allowance and test 0–15 s. (These bounds are my proposal; no search gave the number.)
- Network effect: Seoul found signal delays shrink walksheds by ≈9 % on average and up to 24 % (S5-18), so ignoring crossings overstates LRT catchments noticeably where stops sit beside multi-lane arterials (relevant to the Krayot corridor).

### 2.3 Circuity factor
- Searches gave 1.21–1.23 for transit-station catchments (S5-21, source unidentified) and the qualitative finding that walking networks are less circuitous than driving networks (S5-20) and that actual commutes are less circuitous than random pairs (S5-19). No search surfaced a value above 1.3 for pedestrian networks.
- Recommendation: avoid the factor altogether by routing on the OSM pedestrian network including steps (Section 3). Where a buffer is unavoidable, use 1.25 with a sensitivity range 1.2–1.4; on the Carmel slopes the street network is highly circuitous (switchbacks) but the staircases cut across it, so circuity depends strongly on whether steps are in the graph. Note that a slope-aware *time* catchment already differs from a distance catchment; do not apply both a circuity factor and a slope penalty to a Euclidean distance.

### 2.4 Rail/LRT vs bus catchments and the walk-weight interpretation
Consistent evidence that people walk roughly twice as far to rail as to bus (all search-summary, unverified): Sydney medians 749 vs 364 m (S5-23); Brisbane medians 890 vs 440 m, 85th 1.57 vs 1.07 km (S5-25); Montreal 85th 1,259 vs 524 m (S5-24); Calgary LRT suburban mean 649 m / 75th 840 m with the authors stating bus standards would understate LRT distances by about half (S5-22). The derived rail:bus ratios are ≈2.0–2.4 for medians/85th. The counter-example is Melbourne (S5-26): train 85th 1.10 km vs bus 0.99 km (ratio ≈1.1) and tram only 0.71 km, i.e. a street-running tram with close stop spacing attracts bus-like walks.

Interpretation for the model: Daniels & Mulley explicitly attribute the gap to supply (stop spacing, network density), not to traveller characteristics (S5-23). Longer observed walks therefore do not by themselves prove a lower disutility per walk-minute for rail; they are consistent with (i) wider spacing forcing longer walks and (ii) rail's higher service utility (speed, frequency, reliability) making a longer walk worth it. In a nested logit that already carries mode constants and IVT/headway terms, the clean treatment is: same walk-time coefficient for LRT and bus access (walk ≈2× IVT, within the 2–3× range in S5-36), with the LRT catchment cut-off set from rail evidence (85th percentile ≈1.0–1.3 km network distance, vs ≈0.5–1.0 km for bus), and a sensitivity run with the LRT walk weight at ≈0.8× the bus weight to represent the "willing to walk farther" hypothesis. For Nofit's tram-train (41 km, 20 stops ≈2 km spacing, S5-38) the rail-type evidence (Calgary, Montreal commuter rail) is the better analogue than Melbourne's tram. In Haifa, express catchments in slope-aware minutes rather than metres: a 1.2 km flat 85th percentile is ≈15 min at 1.3 m/s, and that same 15 min reaches only ≈850 m uphill at 10 % (derived from 2.1).

### 2.5 Heat and shade
Only a few weights exist: distance in shade perceived as 0.86 × distance in sun, with building shade more effective than trees (S5-34). Haifa's August conditions (31.4 °C mean high, 82 % RH, heat-index 43 °C; S5-38) justify a summer scenario where unshaded walk time is weighted 1/0.86 ≈ 1.16 relative to shaded (derived). This needs a building-shadow or canopy layer and is a second-order refinement after slope and stairs.

## 3. Short method: slope-aware walk access times in Haifa from OSM + DEM

1. **Network**: `osmnx.graph_from_place/polygon` with `network_type="walk"`, keeping `highway=steps` (tag each edge `is_steps`). Check that Haifa's informal staircases are mapped; add missing ones from the municipality's stairs inventory if available.
2. **DEM**: Copernicus GLO-30 (free; S5-40) as a start, but it is a *surface* model, so sample node elevations after masking buildings or, better, obtain the Survey of Israel DTM (request via MAPI/govmap). Reproject to EPSG:4326 for r5 or keep ITM (EPSG:2039) for OSMnx raster sampling.
3. **Elevations and grades**: `ox.elevation.add_node_elevations_raster(G, dem.tif)` then `ox.elevation.add_edge_grades(G)` (verified API, S5-39). For long or curved edges, split edges at ≈25–30 m before sampling so a single grade does not average out a switchback.
4. **Edge time**: for non-step edges, `time = length / (v_flat × tobler_norm(grade))` with the downhill cap of Section 2.1; for step edges use Fruin stair speeds along the slope (up ≈0.55 m/s, down ≈0.75 m/s) and, in the behavioural layer, the 1.86 perceived-time multiplier. Store `time_up`, `time_down` as directed attributes (grade sign flips with direction).
5. **Crossings**: identify `highway=crossing` nodes; add `crossing=traffic_signals` → 25–30 s, `crossing=uncontrolled/zebra` → 5–10 s, no crossing on a primary/secondary road → treat as a barrier or 60 s. Implement as node penalties on the directed edges entering the crossing.
6. **Routing**: `networkx` shortest paths with weight `time_*` from each LRT stop node (Dijkstra, cut-off 20 min) to centroids/addresses; export isochrones (5/10/15 min) and per-zone access time for the mode-choice model. Alternatively build the same thing in r5r/r5py with `elevation_cost_function=TOBLER` and the DEM `.tif` in the data folder (verified option, S5-39); r5 handles the normalised Tobler averaging per edge but not stair speeds or crossing penalties, so apply those as OSM edge-speed overrides or post-hoc.
7. **Outputs for the parameter review**: per stop, population within 5/10/15 slope-aware minutes vs within 400/800/1,200 m Euclidean; the ratio is the Haifa-specific "catchment shrink factor" to replace generic circuity and slope assumptions. Report sensitivities: v_flat ∈ {1.0, 1.2, 1.3}, slope function ∈ {Tobler-capped, Tobler, Naismith}, signal delay ∈ {15, 30, 40} s.
8. **Validation when data arrive**: compare against observed Carmelit/bus boardings by stop, and against the Technion effort-based model's Haifa case studies (S5-37) if the authors' trajectories can be obtained.
